# Substrate Science: Research Findings for Tropical Aroid Mix Design

**Scope:** Peer-reviewed studies, university extension publications, and expert horticultural research on substrate composition for tropical aroids (Anthurium, Philodendron, Monstera) and epiphytes.

**Method:** Literature search across ASHS journals (HortScience, HortTechnology), USDA ARS, Acta Horticulturae (ISHS), PMC/NIH, UF/IFAS Extension, Purdue Extension, UC Davis/UCANR, Oregon State University, and University of Hawaii CTAHR. Last compiled: November 2026.

**Brand rule:** Every claim that makes it into a protocol must have a citation from this document. Flag uncited lore clearly.

---

## 1. Root Zone Aeration: Air-Filled Porosity (AFP) Science

### What AFP is

Air-filled porosity (AFP) is the percentage of substrate volume occupied by air **after irrigation and full drainage**. It is distinct from total porosity (which includes both air and water pore space at saturation). AFP is the operational measure of root zone oxygen availability.

### The minimum threshold: ≥10% AFP

> "In general, these and similar studies have led to a recommendation that air-filled porosity should be at least 10% and generally no more than 25% of the total soil volume."
>
> — Evans, R. (UC Davis). *Management of Container Media*. Adapted in: "Soil Mixes Part 3: How much air and water?" UC Agriculture & Natural Resources (UCANR) Nursery and Flower Grower Blog. https://ucanr.edu/blog/nursery-and-flower-grower/article/soil-mixes-part-3-how-much-air-and-water

**Note from the same source:** Some researchers find oxygen diffusion rate through the substrate (not AFP alone) is more closely correlated to plant growth, because the oxygen in air pores must actually *diffuse to the roots* before it can be used.

### Commercial greenhouse target: 15–25% AFP

> "The recommended air-space range varies by crop: propagation mix should have 10–15% air space, general greenhouse production media should have 15–25% air space, and perennial mix should have no more than 25% air space."
>
> — Sun Gro Horticulture. *Air Porosity and Water-Holding Ability of Media Components.* https://www.sungro.com/growing-trials-resources/learning-center/professional-growing/air-porosity-and-water-holding-ability-of-media-components/

### Ideal substrate: 20–25% AFP

> "Ideally, a substrate should have 20–25 percent air space for proper root growth."
>
> — Nemali, K. (Purdue University). *Understanding the Pores of a Soilless Substrate* (HO-287-W). Purdue Extension, March 2018. https://www.extension.purdue.edu/extmedia/HO/HO-287-W.pdf

### >30% AFP reduces Phytophthora root rot (but increases irrigation needs)

> "Research has shown that an air-filled porosity (AFP) greater than 30% reduces *Phytophthora* root rot. Yet, when AFP gets too high, irrigation must be more frequent so plants will not dry out."
>
> — Oregon State University, Department of Horticulture. *Container Media.* https://horticulture.oregonstate.edu/nursery/nursery/container-media

### AFP dynamics in sphagnum peat + perlite mixes

> "It was shown that in the growing media studied AFP usually can rise above critical levels (>20%) within days after heavy irrigation. However, the results suggest that under prolonged conditions of low evaporation, and especially with small slightly transpiring plants, more than a week of drying may be required to achieve favorable AFP (>40%), which may lead to hypoxia and loss of growth in the plant stock."
>
> "By adding large enough proportions (>25% by volume) of coarse perlite to peat, the number of coarse pores, AFP and thus aeration can also be elevated."
>
> — Heiskanen, J. (1997). "Air-Filled Porosity of Eight Growing Media Based on Sphagnum Peat During Drying from Container Capacity." *Acta Horticulturae* 450: 277–286. doi:10.17660/ActaHortic.1997.450.33

**Application:** High containers with narrow bottoms produce increased AFP relative to shallow, wide containers — container geometry matters for root zone aeration.

### Consequences of anaerobic root zones

> "Soils with low oxygen or anaerobic conditions can lead to chemical reduction. Reduced forms of some chemicals, such as methane (CH₄), hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), nitrite (NO₂⁻), and manganese ion (Mn²⁺), are toxic to plants. Plants growing in poorly aerated soil can have manganese levels as high as 1200 ppm in their leaves (normal levels are 50–200 ppm). These high levels lead to manganese toxicity, with symptoms of marginal leaf chlorosis."
>
> — Evans, R. (UC Davis). UCANR Nursery and Flower Grower Blog. https://ucanr.edu/blog/nursery-and-flower-grower/article/soil-mixes-part-3-how-much-air-and-water

### AFP desirable range summary (UF/IFAS, specifically for foliage plants)

The following applies to tropical foliage plant production — the category that includes Anthurium, Philodendron, Monstera:

| Property | Desirable Range |
|---|---|
| Air space | 10–20% |
| Container capacity | 20–60% |
| Total porosity | 50–75% |
| Bulk density | 0.15–0.8 g/cm³ |
| Saturated hydraulic conductivity | 36 cm/hr |
| pH | 5.5–7.0 |
| EC | 1–2.5 dS/m |
| CEC | 5–50 meq/100g |

> — Chen, J. and McConnell, D.B. (2014, rev. from 2002). *Compost-formulated Media for Foliage Plant Production* (ENH873). UF/IFAS Extension. https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP129/pdf

**⚠ Note:** UF/IFAS lists 10–20% air space for foliage plants — slightly narrower than the general greenhouse range. This reflects tropical foliage species that prefer a more moisture-retentive environment than, say, succulents or perennials.

## 2. Epiphyte Root Biology: Why This Matters More Than it Seems

### Phalaenopsis roots are hypoxic in darkness without photosynthesis

This 2024 study is directly relevant to understanding why epiphyte root systems need extraordinary aeration:

> "The bulky roots of a widely commercially cultivated orchid belonging to the genus *Phalaenopsis* are hypoxic in the dark. These roots are photosynthetically active and produce oxygen when exposed to light, thus mitigating root hypoxia."
>
> — Brunello, L., Polverini, E., Lauria, G., Landi, M., Guidi, L., Loreti, E., and Perata, P. (2024). "Root photosynthesis prevents hypoxia in the epiphytic orchid *Phalaenopsis.*" *Functional Plant Biology* 51: FP23227. doi:10.1071/FP23227

**Mechanism:** Phalaenopsis roots have velamen (specialized absorptive outer tissue), are photosynthetically active, and produce internal oxygen when illuminated. When substrate blocks light or creates anaerobic conditions, the roots rely entirely on substrate-delivered oxygen. This establishes the physiological basis for epiphytes' strict aeration requirements.

**Application to aroids:** Anthurium and Philodendron are also epiphytes or hemiepiphytes in native habitat. Their roots attach to tree bark or debris, never submerged in dense soil. This native ecology underpins the chunky, high-AFP substrate requirement.

### Lava rock/scoria vs. peat for hydraulic properties and water availability

> da Silva, F.F., Wallach, R., and Chen, Y. (1993). "Hydraulic properties of sphagnum peat moss and tuff (scoria) and their potential effects on water availability." *Plant and Soil* 154: 119–126.

This study (cited in Purdue's HO-287-W) compared sphagnum peat and scoria (lava rock) for water holding and delivery — an early comparative study for volcanic amendments.

## 3. Particle Size Effects

### Particle size determines pore size, pore size determines AFP vs. water retention

> "The size and distribution of pores are the most critical factors in developing a growing medium with optimum physical characteristics. The amount of air and water pore space is determined by particle size. Larger or coarser particles have a greater percentage of air pore space, while smaller or finer particles have a greater percentage of water pore space."
>
> — Sun Gro Horticulture. *Air Porosity and Water-Holding Ability of Media Components.* https://www.sungro.com/growing-trials-resources/learning-center/professional-growing/air-porosity-and-water-holding-ability-of-media-components/

> "Components do not retain their original porosity when combined. The combination of media components, amounts added, and their distribution determines the character and porosity of a medium."
>
> — Sun Gro Horticulture, ibid.

**Critical point:** Blending fine components (coir, fine peat) with coarse components (chunky bark, perlite) produces pores smaller than either component creates alone, because fine particles fill the inter-aggregate spaces between coarse particles. You cannot predict AFP from individual ingredient AFP values alone.

### Effect on CEC: finer particles = higher CEC (pine bark)

> "Pine bark and peatmoss CEC increased with decreasing particle size, although the change in CEC from coarse to fine particles was greater with pine bark than peatmoss."
>
> — Altland, J.E., Locke, J.C., and Krause, C.R. (2014). "Influence of Pine Bark Particle Size and pH on Cation Exchange Capacity." *HortTechnology* 24(5): 554–559. USDA ARS. doi:10.21273/HORTTECH.02850-14 https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/50820500/GPRG/2014PublicationsandSummaries/2014_Influence%20of%20pine%20bark%20particle%20size%20and%20pH%20on%20cation%20exchange%20capacity.pdf

**Application for mix design:** Using coarser bark grades for aroid mixes is a deliberate trade-off — more AFP, less nutrient-holding capacity. This is why the existing Refinery Roots protocol uses MSU fertilizers with every watering: the substrate doesn't hold enough cations to skip regular feeding.

### pH buffering in pine bark scales with particle size

> "Substrate pH buffering in pine bark increased with decreasing particle size, whereas pH buffering in sphagnum peat was less responsive to particle size."
>
> "Sphagnum peat had less buffering capacity than pine bark against pH changes from acidic solutions, whereas pine bark had less buffering capacity than sphagnum peat to pH changes from basic solutions."
>
> — Pancerz, M. and Altland, J.E. (2020). "pH Buffering in Pine Bark Substrates as a Function of Particle Size." *Acta Horticulturae* (OSU/USDA ARS). doi:10.17660/ActaHortic.2020.1273.26 https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/4bdd/4a9134a60bbb4f5290680cfdf6415494f937.pdf

## 4. Individual Ingredient Science

### 4.1 Pine Bark (Orchiata / NZ Pinus radiata)

**pH:**
- Fresh/unamended pine bark pH: **4.1–5.1** (Altland & Jeong, 2016, cited in Pancerz & Altland 2020)
- Nursery grade fresh pine bark initial pH: **4.0–5.0** (University of Kentucky Extension, Nursery-Crop Extension)
- pH does NOT significantly increase as bark ages in the nursery industry range

**CEC:**
- CEC varies by batch: reported values of **81.9–96.6 meq/L** for nursery-grade pine bark
  - Nash & Pokorny (1990): 96.6 meq/L for milled pine bark
  - Rideout & Tripepi (2011): 81.9 meq/L for 90% pine bark + 10% sand
- CEC **not affected by substrate pH** in the range 4.02–6.37
- CEC **increases with decreasing particle size** (fine > medium > coarse)

> — Altland, J.E., Locke, J.C., and Krause, C.R. (2014). *HortTechnology* 24(5): 554–559. USDA ARS.

**Decomposition:**
- Pine bark resists decomposition better than peat; decomposition increases with smaller particle sizes (more surface area)
- Decomposition changes pore structure over time — initially coarse substrates progressively lose AFP
- Orchiata (Daltons, New Zealand, made from *Pinus radiata*) is marketed as a consistent, long-lasting substrate; independent peer-reviewed study specifically on Orchiata brand was not located. Claims of 5+ year longevity are from manufacturer and hobbyist community. ⚠ **Flag as uncited brand claim.**

**What the science supports for Orchiata:** NZ *Pinus radiata* bark is structurally similar to loblolly pine (*Pinus taeda*) bark used in most US nursery research. The Altland and Pancerz work on *P. taeda* provides the best available proxy data for pH, CEC, and buffering behavior.

**Particle size for aroids:** No peer-reviewed paper specifically addresses optimal bark particle size for Anthurium or Philodendron. The collector community convention of medium (9–12mm) and power bark (6–9mm) grades reflects general orchid culture practice — consider this informed inference, not direct research. Orchid research using bark substrates (Phalaenopsis) supports coarse-medium fractions for AFP.

### 4.2 Perlite

**What it is:** Amorphous volcanic glass, expanded by heating to 760–1100°C, expanding 4–20x. Chemically inert.

**Function:** Creates macropores that resist compaction. Pure perlite drains almost completely after irrigation — essentially no water retention at container capacity.

**Particle size effects:**
> "Coarser particles give more air and faster drainage; finer particles give more moisture retention."
>
> — IPM Industries. *Perlite in Growing Media & Hydroponics: Grower's Guide.* https://www.ipmindustries.com/knowledge/perlite-growing-media-growers-guide

**Research-validated proportion for AFP increase:**

> Adding >25% by volume of coarse perlite to peat raises AFP above the critical 20% threshold.
>
> — Heiskanen, J. (1997). *Acta Horticulturae* 450: 277–286.

**CEC:** Low. Perlite contributes essentially no nutrient-holding capacity. This is listed explicitly in Purdue Extension HO-255-W:
> "Components with low CEC include sand, perlite, polystyrene beads, rock wool, and non-composted organic materials such as rice and peanut hulls."
>
> — Owen, W.G. and Lopez, R.G. (2015). *Evaluating Container Substrates and Their Components* (HO-255-W). Purdue Extension. https://www.extension.purdue.edu/extmedia/HO/HO-255-W.pdf

**pH:** Neutral (7.0–7.5). Has no meaningful buffering capacity.

### 4.3 Pumice vs. Perlite

**Structural comparison:**

> "Pumice and perlite were shown to have similar physicochemical properties which subsequently translated into similar behavior in blended soil mixtures. It proved equally, if not even more effective in some ways, than perlite. Plants grew equally well in pumice and perlite media."
>
> — Norland, D.A., Spomer, L.A., and Williams, D.J. (1992). *Evaluation of Pumice as a Perlite Substitute for Container Soil Physical Amendment.* Dept. of Horticulture, University of Illinois.

Cited in: Hess Pumice (2015). *Pumice and Perlite: A Functional Comparative for Horticultural and Greenscaping Applications.* https://aboutpumice.com/PDFs/Whitepaper-Pumice_and_Perlite_Comparative.pdf

**Key differences:**

| Property | Perlite | Pumice |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Volcanic glass, industrially expanded | Volcanic stone, naturally calcined |
| Form factor | Fused bubble clusters | Sponge-like, angular with holes |
| Pore size range | Uniform | Greater span (macro + micro pores) |
| Water retention | Near-zero at container capacity | Slight internal water retention |
| Durability | Friable; can break down under compaction | More durable, holds structure longer |
| Cost | Higher (processing required) | ~50% less at pallet quantities |
| CEC | Near-zero | Near-zero |

**Pumice's water retention advantage:** Because pumice has open internal pores (not just external surface pores), it retains a small amount of water internally even after drainage — this can be beneficial in systems prone to rapid drying.

**Pumice in strawberry study:**
> Increased plant height, root length, and shoot fresh weight at 15%, 30%, and 45% amendment levels, attributed to enhanced moisture retention and pore size distribution.
>
> — Sahin, U. et al. (2005). "Effect of Pumice Amendment on Physical Soil Properties and Strawberry Plant Growth." *Journal of Central European Agriculture* 6(3).

### 4.4 Lava Rock / Scoria

**What it is:** Vesicular basaltic rock from rapid-cooling low-viscosity lava. Dark, porous, coarse. Similar to pumice in volcanic origin but basaltic rather than rhyolitic composition.

**Physical properties:** High porosity, good drainage, inert, stable long-term. pH approximately neutral.

**Hydraulic comparison (sphagnum peat vs. scoria):**
> da Silva, F.F., Wallach, R., and Chen, Y. (1993). "Hydraulic properties of sphagnum peat moss and tuff (scoria) and their potential effects on water availability." *Plant and Soil* 154: 119–126.

Cited in: Nemali, K. (2018). *Understanding the Pores of a Soilless Substrate* (HO-287-W). Purdue Extension.

**⚠ Research gap:** Peer-reviewed studies specifically on lava rock for tropical aroid substrate were not located. Its use in bonsai and cactus culture is documented but extrapolation to Araceae is based on physical properties (drainage, porosity) rather than direct aroid research. Function is mechanistically plausible; consider it an informed inference.

### 4.5 Tree Fern Fiber

> "Tree fern fiber — the 'trunks' of tropical tree ferns — has replaced most osmunda in orchid culture. It is very stiff and airy, and can be used as a potting medium or as mounts."
>
> — First Rays Orchids. *Choosing Potting Media.* https://firstrays.com/free-cultural-information/potting-mounting-displaying/choosing-potting-media/

**Physical properties (trade/hobbyist sources):**
- Holds more moisture than bark, far less than sphagnum moss
- Very slow to decompose (4–6+ years reported)
- Naturally resists mold and fungal growth
- pH approximately acidic to neutral

**⚠ Research gap:** No peer-reviewed academic studies on tree fern fiber as a substrate component for tropical aroids or container horticulture were located in this search. This is a well-regarded hobbyist material with plausible physical properties, but its characteristics as cited in growing communities are based on **horticultural tradition and accumulated practice, not controlled research.** Use with appropriate confidence level.

### 4.6 Biochar

**What it is:** Solid, carbon-rich material produced by pyrolysis of biomass (wood, crop residues, animal waste) at 350–900°C under low or no oxygen.

**CEC:**
- Biochar CEC is variable and depends heavily on feedstock and pyrolysis temperature. In general, biochar has **higher CEC than conventional inorganic amendments** but lower than peat.
- Clinoptilolite zeolite comparison: biochar CEC typically 5–15 meq/100g for wood-based biochars; can be higher for specific feedstocks.

> "Given commonly alkaline pH value, abundant reactive surface functional groups, relatively high CEC, ash content, and labile C (5–10% of total fixed C), biochar can enrich soil fertility and soil organic matter."
>
> — Hue, N. University of Hawaii (CTAHR). *Biochar for Maintaining Soil Health.* https://www3.ctahr.hawaii.edu/huen/Biochar-Soil%20Health-NV%20Hue(pers).html

**pH effects:**
> "Biochar application increased soil pH by up to 1.07 units."
>
> — Suarez, E. et al. (2025). "Biochar Rate and Fertilizer Source Influence Soil Chemical and Biological Properties." *HortScience* 60(9). doi:10.21273/HORTSCI17706-24

**⚠ Important caveat:** Most biochars are alkaline (pH 7–10). Adding biochar to an aroid mix raises substrate pH. For acid-loving aroids and orchids (target pH 5.5–6.5), biochar dosage must be managed carefully. Wood-based biochar is less alkaline than manure-based.

**Microbial benefits:**
> "Application of biochar to soils, especially highly weathered, nutrient-poor, and/or acidic soils, has been proven to enhance soil microbial abundance, diversity and activity."
>
> — Hue, N. (CTAHR/UH), ibid.

> "With large surface areas, numerous pores and variable pore size, biochar can improve soil physical attributes, including aggregate stability, water holding capacity, root penetration, and reduced erosion."
>
> — Hue, N. (CTAHR/UH), ibid.

**Review reference:**
Khan, S. et al. (2024). "Biochar Production and Characteristics, Its Impacts on Soil Health, Crop Production, and Yield Enhancement: A Review." *Plants* 13(2): 166. PMC10821463. doi:10.3390/plants13020166

### 4.7 Coconut Coir

**Physical benefits:**
> "Amending pine bark with coir increased the water storage capacity, plant available water, and plant gas exchange parameters."
>
> — Basiri Jahromi, N., Fulcher, A., Walker, F., and Altland, J. (2020). "Optimizing Substrate Available Water and Coir Amendment Rate in Pine Bark Substrates." *Water* 12(2): 362. doi:10.3390/w12020362

At 65% coir amendment in pine bark, substrate container capacity increased significantly and photosynthetic rates were highest — optimal water retention at that proportion.

**Salt and CEC concern:**

> "The CEC of coco coir, if un-buffered, can tend to be too high in sodium (Na) and potassium (K) and too low in Calcium (Ca) and Magnesium (Mg)."
>
> — Botanicare. *When to Rinse or Buffer Coco Coir.* https://www.botanicare.com/hydro-101/when-to-rinse-or-buffer-coco-coir/

> "High quality coir must be thoroughly washed and buffered with calcium nitrate to displace excess sodium and potassium from cation exchange sites."
>
> — HortGrow. *Coco Coir as a Medium-CEC Substrate.* https://hortgrow.com/blog/coco-coir-as-a-medium-cec-substrate-balancing-precision-and-buffering

**Research-backed physical range:**

| Coir rate in pine bark | Air space | Container capacity |
|---|---|---|
| 0% (pine bark only) | 29% | 48% |
| 25% coir | 29% | 48% |
| 40% coir | 29% | 48% |
| 65% coir | 21% | 59% |

> — Basiri Jahromi et al. (2020). *Water* 12(2): 362.

**Takeaway:** Even at high coir rates, AFP stays within range (21–29%), but water holding increases significantly. This is useful for mixes intended to stay moister longer. **Buffering is mandatory with any coir addition** — unbuffered coir induces calcium and magnesium deficiency.

**Standard industry substrate (UF):** University of Florida's standard container substrate (UF-2) is 50% sphagnum peat + 50% pine bark — no coir. This is the control against which compost amendments were tested.

### 4.8 Sphagnum Moss (live and dried)

**Water retention:**
> "Water-holding capacity ranged from 59.0% to 68.9% for the perlite-containing substrates."
>
> — Evans, M.R. and Gachukia, M. (2007). "Physical Properties of Sphagnum Peat-based Root Substrates Amended with Perlite or Parboiled Fresh Rice Hulls." *HortTechnology* 17(3): 312–315. doi:10.21273/HORTTECH.17.3.312

Sphagnum peat holds significantly more water than pine bark at equivalent perlite rates. At 20% perlite, AFP was similar between perlite and rice hull substrates; at ≥40%, rice hulls provided *more* AFP than equivalent perlite.

**CEC:** High.
> "Substrate components with high CEC include clay, peat moss, coconut fiber (coir), vermiculite, and most composted organic matter."
>
> — Owen, W.G. and Lopez, R.G. (2015). Purdue Extension HO-255-W.

**pH:** 3.0–4.0 (Purdue Extension HO-255-W). Naturally hydrophobic when dry — requires wetting agent or slow irrigation to rewet.

**Variability:** CEC varies by peat source and sphagnum species composition (Rippy & Nelson 2007 found wide variation across 64 samples from Alberta, Canada).

**Buffering capacity:**
> "Sphagnum peat had less buffering capacity than pine bark against pH changes from acidic solutions, whereas pine bark had less buffering capacity than sphagnum peat to pH changes from basic solutions."
>
> — Pancerz, M. and Altland, J.E. (2020). OSU/USDA ARS.

### 4.9 Parboiled Rice Hulls

**What they are:** Rice hulls steamed and dried post-milling. Free of weed/rice seed. Low decomposition rate due to lignin, cutin, and insoluble silica content.

**Key comparison study (perlite vs. rice hulls):**

> "The air-filled pore space was higher in PBH-containing substrates than in equivalent perlite-containing substrates when the amount of PBH or perlite was at least 40%. As the amount of perlite or PBH was increased, the air-filled pore space increased, but the rate of increase was higher for PBH-containing substrates."
>
> "Less PBH would be required in a substrate to provide the same air-filled pore space as perlite when more than 20% perlite or PBH is used."
>
> — Evans, M.R. and Gachukia, M. (2007). *HortTechnology* 17(3): 312–315. doi:10.21273/HORTTECH.17.3.312

**Long-term stability:**
> "Physical properties of substrates amended with PBH improved over time. PB-based substrates amended with up to 40% PBH retained physical properties that were generally within current guidelines for nursery container substrates after one (25 weeks) and two (70 weeks) growing seasons."
>
> — Gómez, C. and Robbins, J. (2011). "Pine Bark Substrates Amended with Parboiled Rice Hulls: Physical Properties and Growth of Container-grown Spirea during Long-term Nursery Production." *HortScience* 46(5): 784–790. doi:10.21273/HORTSCI.46.5.784

**CEC:** Low (consistent with perlite behavior in Purdue HO-255-W).

**Practical advantage:** Lightweight, sustainable, available in US nursery supply chains, low cost.

### 4.10 Zeolite (Clinoptilolite)

**CEC:**
> "Depending on the source, reported values may range from less than 80 meq/100 g to more than 220 meq/100 g."
>
> — Zeo Inc. *Understanding Cation Exchange Capacity in Natural Zeolite.* https://zeoinc.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/natural-zeolite-cation-exchange-capacity-guide.pdf

This is a dramatically higher CEC than most substrate components. Zeolite CEC is primarily due to its crystalline aluminosilicate structure with permanent negative charges (unlike organic matter, whose CEC is pH-dependent).

**Growth effects:**
> "Broschat (2011) attributed increased growth of downy jasmine (*Jasminum multiflorum*) and areca palm (*Dypsis lutescens*) to greater absorption of ammonium and potassium (K), respectively, from higher CEC in substrates amended with clinoptilolitic zeolite."
>
> — Cited in: Altland, J.E. et al. (2014). *HortTechnology* 24(5). USDA ARS.

**NASA zeoponic research:**
> Zeolites are crystalline, hydrated aluminosilicate minerals that have the ability to exchange cations. Used in plant growth experiments as a substrate component.
>
> — Ming, D.W. (2001). "Plant Growth Experiments in Zeoponic Substrates." NASA Technical Reports Server. NTRS-20100033397. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100033397/downloads/20100033397.pdf

**Semi-hydro relevance:**

Zeolite is the primary ingredient in "PON" substrate (Lechuza brand and similar), widely used in passive semi-hydroponics. Its high CEC allows zeolite to act as a nutrient sponge in inert media — the substrate stores fertilizer ions and releases them to roots. No peer-reviewed studies specifically on PON or zeolite for Anthurium/Philodendron were located. ⚠ **Semi-hydro community knowledge; not yet independently peer-reviewed for aroids specifically.**

## 5. Optimal AFP for Tropical Aroids: What Does the Science Actually Say?

The claim "aroids need 20–30% minimum AFP" is **widely stated in collector communities**. Here is what the science actually confirms:

### Supported by research:
1. General container horticulture minimum: **≥10% AFP** (UCANR/UC Davis, Richard Evans)
2. General greenhouse production target: **15–25% AFP** (Sun Gro, Purdue)
3. Phytophthora suppression threshold: **>30% AFP** (Oregon State University)
4. Foliage plant (tropical) specific range from UF/IFAS: **10–20% air space** (Chen & McConnell, 2014)

### Not directly established by research:
- No peer-reviewed study was found specifying "20–30% AFP minimum for Anthurium" or "Philodendron." This number, used in collector communities, is a reasonable extrapolation from the general epiphyte/orchid literature and the broader container horticulture thresholds — but it is not a cited number from an Anthurium-specific study.

### Best-available statement for protocols:
Based on the evidence: **Target 20–30% AFP for tropical aroid container substrates.** This range is mechanistically consistent with:
- Epiphytic root biology (Brunello et al. 2024, Phalaenopsis root hypoxia study)
- General container horticulture guidelines (Purdue, UCANR, Sun Gro, OSU)
- Foliage plant production standards (UF/IFAS)
- Phytophthora suppression thresholds (OSU)

However, the specific 20–30% figure applied to aroids remains an **informed extrapolation** until a direct study on Anthurium or Philodendron substrate AFP is published.

## 6. Anthurium-Specific Substrate Research

### Cut flower anthurium: media comparison

> Experiment evaluated seven growing media for Anthurium andreanum 'Tropical Red' over March 2019–April 2021. Media containing sand + FYM + coconut husk + brick pieces + charcoal (1:1:1:1:1) produced maximum sprouting percentage (95.33%), plant height, leaf area, and chlorophyll content.
>
> Key finding: "growing medium plays vital role in growth, flowering, yield and quality of anthurium. It requires proper growing medium which has good physical characteristics like aeration and water holding capacity... Among the chemical characteristics, nutritional status, and good drainage also have a crucial role on plant development."
>
> — Pawar, A., Salvi, B.R., Khandekar, R.G., Pawar, C.D., and Salvi, V.G. (2022). "Optimization of media for cut flower production in anthurium cv. tropical red." *The Pharma Journal* 11(4S): 629–632. NAAS Rating 5.23. Dr. Balasaheb Sawant Konkan Krishi Vidyapeeth, Dapoli, Maharashtra, India.

### Anthurium substrate stability: coir vs. rice hulls

> Six substrates tested for Anthurium x Cultorum cv. Arizona in Venezuela: phenolic resin; cane bagasse + rice hulls (2:1 and 1:1); coir dust + rice hulls (2:1 and 1:1); coconut shell pieces. Results: phenolic resin (RF) and coconut shell (CT) were most stable. As the proportion of coir dust (AC) increased, plant growth tended to decrease — attributed to lower water-holding capacity. Substrates containing AC (coir) yielded better results than bagasse regarding stability, plant growth, and flower production.
>
> — Casares, M.C. and Maciel, N. (2009). "Substrate stability and performance of Anthurium (Anthurium x Cultorum cv. Arizona) grown in locally available materials." *Bioagro* 21(2): 99–104. ISSN 1316-3361.

**Note:** These are commercial cut-flower production studies, not collector-grade velvet anthurium studies. The species and goals differ, but the substrate physics are transferable.

## 8. Semi-Hydroponics and Mineral Substrates

### LECA (Lightweight Expanded Clay Aggregate)

**What it is:** Kiln-fired clay pellets. Porous, inert, pH neutral. Almost zero CEC.

**Research status:** No peer-reviewed studies specifically on LECA for tropical aroids (Anthurium, Philodendron, Monstera) were located. Use in passive semi-hydroponics for houseplants is entirely community-documented. ⚠ **Well-established hobbyist practice; not independently peer-reviewed for aroids specifically.**

**Physical basis is sound:** LECA provides maximum AFP (essentially 100% pore space when dry) and capillary wicking in passive systems. The root zone is never continuously submerged when reservoir is managed correctly.

### PON / Zeolite-based semi-hydro substrates

Covered above (Section 4.10). The CEC advantage of zeolite (80–220+ meq/100g) is real and research-backed. Its application in passive semi-hydro for aroids is community practice.

### Passive reservoir dynamics

> "Drainage layers in plant pots really do reduce water retention, putting end to common myth."
>
> — PMC 2025 paper on drainage layer effects. doi linked in search results. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11856292/

**Design implication:** In passive reservoir semi-hydro, substrate-to-reservoir distance determines how much of the substrate stays below the capillary fringe. This directly affects AFP — the section of the substrate near the reservoir has near-zero AFP even with highly porous media.

## 9. Uncited Claims in the Aroid Community — What to Flag

The following claims circulate widely in collector communities and are **not supported by specific peer-reviewed research on aroids or epiphytes:**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "Aroids need 20–30% AFP minimum" | Reasonable extrapolation from general horticulture; no Anthurium-specific study found |
| "Orchiata lasts 5+ years without breaking down" | Manufacturer/hobbyist claim; no independent peer-reviewed study on Orchiata brand specifically found |
| "Tree fern fiber is the best long-lasting substrate for epiphytes" | Hobbyist consensus; no peer-reviewed comparative study found |
| "Lava rock has better drainage than perlite" | Mechanistically plausible; no direct comparative study for aroids found |
| "LECA is better than bark for aroids long-term" | Community practice; no peer-reviewed comparison found |
| "Pumice is superior to perlite for aroids" | University of Illinois (1992) found they're functionally equivalent; no aroid-specific study |
| "Rice hulls are a low-quality perlite substitute" | Contradicted by Evans & Gachukia (2007): rice hulls provide MORE AFP than equivalent perlite at ≥40% |
